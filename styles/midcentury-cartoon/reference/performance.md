# Performance calibration for a new subject

The reusable unit is a performed explanation: an actor or object acts, its state
changes, the camera makes that change legible, and the viewer has time to read
the result. A small presenter beside a static chart does not inherit the
reference quality merely because the palette and fonts match.

## Drawing and staging

Compare original frames 74, 210, 455, 588 and 692. They change ground, scale and
viewpoint while preserving print treatment. Choose a dominant paper, mustard,
dusty blue, avocado or pink ground per shot. Ground changes follow changes of
function; do not randomly cycle colours. Prop colours stay stable across shots.

In performance shots, the actor and operated prop occupy the reading area.
In detail shots, the operated surface dominates. In overhead shots, the
mechanism fills the space. Size by what the viewer must see. Recurring headers,
empty cards, logo bars and progress lines should not dictate every composition.
Titles enter when needed. Captions use a fitted bottom paper plaque.

Inspect native line/fill boundaries, face, sleeve, wrist and fingers. The fill
is slightly offset from pressure-varying, partially broken ink. Paper remains
stationary. Thin screen-space ink survives close framing; fingers enlarged into
black pipes need redrawing or scale compensation. Decode the source using its
declared colour tags; a conversion mistake can look like a palette difference.

When enlarging a prop, recompute its contact target from the enlarged geometry.
Compensate both outline width and colour-registration displacement in screen
space; compensating ink alone can leave an oversized double contour. Check
the active control, the state label, the hand's full route and the released
result against the caption band. Compare the operated surface's visible size
with a function-matched reference, rather than enlarging the hand alone.

## Causal action phases

| Phase | Evidence in the shot contract |
| --- | --- |
| Anticipation | Visible target; hand begins at a plausible rest/preparation pose |
| Contact | Fingertip, palm, grip or tool tip reaches a declared prop point |
| Operation | Prop and hand stay coupled while pressing, turning, lifting or writing |
| Result | A specific domain state changes after the operation |
| Settle/read | Hand releases or holds deliberately; result remains readable |

Sketch those poses before interpolating. In the original hand detail, f455 and
f489 still show a gap above the control; f503–516 show contact and the orange
feedback ring. At f525 the object-centred iris begins: this is a transition,
not a hand-release pose. Inspect the actual target contact rather than assuming
that a pointing hand is touching it. In the material action, f680, f684 and f692
show the progression toward the lifted object. These are examples, not durations
to copy mechanically into every story. An intentional transition can conclude
a held action; it should not be described as a visible withdrawal.

Use a reachable arm rig. Move or lower the body when the target is out of reach;
do not elongate the forearm to reach a remote label. Share the target and prop
state with the detail window and sound event. Check both orientations and
transition edges. A generic point cannot replace a press, grip, mark or visible
comparison. Bodies are held; selected hands, eyes, mouths and props move. Poses
and registration update on twos, inside 24 fps; camera/masks may update every
frame. Constant bobbing is not limited animation. A held result is purposeful;
a whole chapter with no performed change is a different problem.

## Shot grammar and time

Use establishment → operation/detail → consequence → read/reaction where the
explanation calls for it. Split long narration at semantic turns. A changed
caption alone is not a changed shot. The registered short example has 23 shots
in 198 seconds; the long example has 65 in 625.5 seconds. These are observed
cadences, not quotas. A long film needs close actions, overhead mechanisms,
comparisons, reaction and recall distributed according to its argument.

Compare relative scale and visible information, not just cut counts. Avoid a
repeating presenter/panel arrangement in every chapter. A complex scientific
diagram can need a longer hold; show a progression or an observation task.
Read actual waveforms and word timings. Reserve preparation/result holds,
questions and mechanism pauses. Keep natural speech. If narration leaves no
visual breathing room, remove redundant wording or restage across meaningful
shots; do not fill the film with idle motion or stretch audio to fit runtime.
A musical beat or J-cut can bridge shots without pausing every sentence.

Judge a hold by what remains available to inspect. In the long reference,
frames 1824–2003 keep a result and its qualification visible; 2004–2220 change
to a contrasting situation and reveal its result; 2232–2399 assemble the two
outcomes side by side. The completed drawings then hold. Compare that sequence
with the new film's semantic turns. Do not impose constant motion or use the
mean shot length as a quality score. Conversely, two adjacent narration units
that ask different questions should not inherit the same picture merely by
changing a small symbol. Stage the new question, mechanism or comparison.

When narration changes from one kind of check or question to another, change
what the viewer can inspect. A successful machine indicator should not remain
the sole picture while the voice introduces human interpretation. Use an
appropriate detail, surface or actor view, and time the change to the actual
spoken turn. Keep an unresolved question visibly unresolved. A source note can
sit beside the evidence; a second large plaque that repeats the caption adds
reading load without adding an argument.

Update the reference anchors when a revision changes a shot's function. A pair
of comparison boards is not an adequate performance reference for a newly
inserted step-by-step mechanism. Name the aspect actually borrowed (for example,
progressive links or an enlarged reading surface), and record aspects that are
not matched. Source-frame presence does not establish full visual equivalence.

Separate an explanation of several questions from the final compact recap.
Give each explanation the earlier prop, operation or evidence it recalls, then
collect recognizable miniatures into the closing overview. Pip frames 745–879
and long-reference frames 14064–14268 show this use of actual story objects.
They are references for recall structure, not a mandate to copy their cast,
text or number of items. Reveal or emphasize an item at its spoken cue, and
allow a quiet reading hold when the narration explicitly invites one. A fixed
category colour is not an active-state indicator; record that distinction
before diagnosing a focus error. Only draw connecting arrows when an order or
relationship is intended. Keep badges clear of the miniature's meaningful
parts, and check those miniature layouts separately from their full-size props.

When inserting a shot during revision, preserve the intended transitions on
the other shots explicitly. A transition chosen from a shot's array index can
change throughout the rest of the film after one insertion. Verify unchanged
shot timing and transition choice, then recheck the revised interval and its
neighbours in the encoded film.

## Camera, transitions and sound

An inset keeps context while revealing a control or mark. Redraw at the local
screen scale, and share object state between inset and main view. An iris has
an outgoing semantic center and an incoming reveal. Plane wipes can change
ground/viewpoint. Do not advance an incoming scene before its start or run the
outgoing scene past its final frame.

Attach material sound to the drawing's actual contact/result event. Paper,
switches and pencil marks need different sounds; a musical ping is not every
material. Sparse cool jazz supports narration. Over a long runtime, vary
instrumentation, duck speech and leave quiet observation spans. Judge the actual
mix with its pictures. Loudness numbers cannot validate phrasing or pacing.

## Evidence another agent can use

Record source/candidate identities, matched roles, displayed frame sizes,
actual motion/audio coverage, defects, repairs and remaining concerns. Reject
calibration when a hand misses its prop, text covers an action, an inset changes
object state, lines get too heavy, a ground hides the subject, or the result
appears before the explained action. Recheck repairs and adjacent transitions.

At full length compare shot functions, reading holds, operations and grounds
against the long reference. Pixel distances do not certify aesthetic
equivalence across subjects. Extraction, rendering, ASR and generated sheets
cannot become “100% style matched.” Also distinguish another agent actually
using this kit successfully from a kit merely written and validated.

## Common failures that still frames can miss

A hand appearing directly on its target is a missing anticipation phase. Its
first visible pixels should enter from a plausible rest pose or the frame edge;
its last visible pixels should leave or settle before it is omitted. Inspect
every frame around visibility conditions, not only the contact and result.
Route sleeves around headings for the entire path, including release.

Test the font's actual glyph coverage. A renderer can succeed while printing
an empty square or nothing for superscripts, inequalities or infinity. Use an
explicit supported math face or compose exponents as positioned text. Strip
decorative ruled lines from sheets that receive separately composed equations.

When simplifying narration, preserve its argument and claim qualifiers, then
retime audio, captions, scene boundaries and semantic word markers together.
Reusing old absolute markers after changing speech is a synchronization bug.
Do not trigger a state change while the narrator still describes the prior
state. An explanatory chart needs a shared numerical scale; separately
normalizing different curves can reverse their ordering and misteach a topic.

Before freezing the full render, review a chronological sheet containing every
shot. Distinguish a deliberate continuous reading hold from a new cut that merely
repeats the prior composition. A new ground colour does not make a new visual
argument. Give adjacent explanations their own visible question, operation or
consequence. Verify framing options in rendered images: a field called
`mentor_focus` or `closeup` does nothing unless the renderer actually uses it.
When comparing equations, use the same supported face or composed symbols on
both sides, so a font fallback does not imply a second visual system.

In enumerations and highlighted tables, tie focus to semantic word markers.
Hold the named case for the complete explanation; do not cycle rows simply
because another second elapsed. Advance only when the explanation advances.
Check the highlighted state against narration as well as its caption. Reject
punctuation-only caption cues and merge trailing punctuation into its phrase;
a nonempty string can still be a broken reading unit.

A consequence shot should expose what the preceding comparison changes, rather
than redraw the same comparison under a new heading. Keep left/right identities
and numerical scales stable across the cut. Distinguish a speech-timed attention
cue from a change in the depicted facts: highlighting an excluded case must not
temporarily show it as allowed. Route callouts around tick labels and equations,
then inspect their full entrance path and the transition into the detail.

Give example plates a neutral base unless colour has an explicit, explained
meaning. Reveal each example at its spoken cue; do not let a fixed decorative
colour imply that an earlier example is still the active one. Check the
definition before the first example as its own visual beat: hiding all examples
must not accidentally leave a long, empty title-only screen.

A staged diagram should have a complete composition before its next term is
introduced. Center the first readable object, then make space when the next
term arrives; avoid holding half an empty equation through several seconds of
narration. Couple a growing container and its contents to the same reveal
parameter. Inspect intermediate frames: a child can protrude beyond the panel
while both endpoints look correct. Keep labels and operators separated during
the whole movement, not only in the settled layout.

When a repair changes only a bounded interval, recheck that interval and its
neighbouring transitions in the encoded film. Inherited review is valid only
with evidence that the other frames and timestamps are unchanged. Record the
inspection scale, exact frame ranges and source identity; a contact-sheet tile
does not establish native-resolution detail or realtime motion quality.

When a comparison moves its first object aside, reveal the operator only after
its full ink bounds clear that object. Opacity alone does not solve a moving
collision. A one-object introduction should remain centered until the next
object needs space. Inspect the early, middle and final movement frames, plus
the leading and trailing transitions.

A closing can return to a familiar character and working prop, complete one
last meaningful action, then resolve into a printed end card. In the long
reference, frames 14688–14868 return from the recap to the student and desk;
14858–14880 close around the paper and open the final card. Adapt this function
to the new argument. Do not replace closure with a nearly empty question sheet
held through several distinct spoken ideas. Keep unresolved questions visibly
unresolved; a check mark is a claim, not neutral decoration. Use phrase-based
caption boundaries so a compound word is not cut across consecutive cues.

For opaque paper or card props, a slide-in should preserve opacity so the
background lettering does not show through the moving sheet. If two labels
occupy one baseline, retire the old label before revealing its replacement.
For iris closures, inspect the radius when the edge first enters the picture:
a very large starting radius or nested easing can concentrate nearly all
visible movement into the final few frames. Match the visible movement, not
just the nominal transition duration. Keep language-independent wordmarks in
one explicit font while localizing the surrounding type.


For multilingual versions, derive reveal order from each language's spoken
phrases. A translation may introduce participants before a quantity while the
other language does the reverse. Reuse the visual hierarchy, but record the
semantic cue times independently. Keep announced aims, actual availability and
remaining work distinguishable; a new icon or check mark must not imply an
outcome that the narration does not establish.

Place labels outside the largest transformed ink bounds of a moving prop,
including its outline and print-registration offset. Anchor resting props to
the world-space contact plane, not an approximate visual center. When masking
a row of objects, include the full outline of the first and last objects.
Review the intermediate reveal and replacement frames: a correct settled
composition can hide floating props, clipped circles and transient text overlap.

Check a spoken promise against the next actual explanatory beat. “Start with…”
should lead into that subject, not an intervening chapter about a different
question. If the bridge is misleading, edit the bridge and its matching picture,
then keep any intentional question hold. Place a demonstration beside the words
that explain its states. Removing a spoken clause requires updating its WAV,
caption bounds, timing ledger and material effects together; deleting the text
alone leaves the old voice in the film. Preserve the source recording and document
the cut in a quiet gap. Quantize the shared contact/result cue to the animation's
pose clock so picture and Foley agree, including separately timed translations.

When a physical control and a table depict the same setting, derive the control
pose, state label and selected row from one event/state value. Inspect the
previous state, the intermediate movement and the completed state; a highlighted
row must not claim a result while the corresponding control stays unchanged.
Size mechanism digits and branches for the actual viewing area, with room for
labels and captions. In a branching explanation, use each language's spoken
case to focus the relevant path. Keep a doubling or grouping explanation on
the diagram until the narration asks to read the resulting table. A diagram's
state animation does not imply human contact; document that distinction.

Distinguish an object's current value from the truth of a condition about it.
A positive-looking control is not automatically a passing requirement. Derive
condition marks, grouped results and the overall verdict from the same domain
state, including negated conditions. Use a separate attention treatment for the
clause being narrated; focus must not masquerade as a truth value. An unresolved
decision should retain its question, rather than display a failure or success
that has not been established. When moving between nearby controls, inspect the
entire fingertip and sleeve route: lowering an arc can clear the heading while
introducing a collision with labels below it. Place those labels outside the
whole swept path, and retain contact/result timing when reusing material sound.


For a bent arm, choose the elbow branch in the character’s local coordinate
system and retain it through mirrored poses. Check the visible shoulder-to-elbow
silhouette after torso occlusion: a mathematically reachable wrist can still
look as if the forearm grows from the waist. A resting hand needs its own relaxed
shape; it should not inherit a pointing or tightly closed grip. Keep the bone
segments legible, rounding the elbow locally rather than splining the entire arm
into an S-curve. For an offscreen operator, choose the sleeve’s entry side once;
never flip it by comparing the moving wrist against the entry point each frame.
Inspect entry, exit and both mirrored directions for loops at the picture edge.

When travel feels slow, separate approach, contact hold, active movement, release
and result reading. Shorten travel without accelerating the spoken explanation
or deleting the reading interval. Use brief acceleration and a decisive arrival;
allow small follow-through in a loose prop’s rotation, while keeping the grasp or
contact position coherent. Avoid applying the same slow symmetric ease to every
object. Store action ranges in one cue contract shared by picture and material
sound. If contact completes earlier, move the contact Foley with it; do not
leave a long scrape after the pen or paper has stopped. Inspect intermediate
poses and normal-speed playback separately. A faster numeric curve alone does
not prove natural movement.

The blank starter includes `lib/performance.mjs` for two-bone solving, localized
joint rounding, decisive travel and pose-clock boundaries. These are primitives,
not an accepted actor or an authored timing plan; calibrate their parameters
against the new subject and inspect the resulting silhouette.
