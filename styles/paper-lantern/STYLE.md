# Paper Lightbox

Tell a short story through a change in light. Make the first and last states of a recognizable physical object legible. Design the object with a silhouette, a few interior incisions and a feature that can move on its own pivot. Find a wide that establishes where it is, a travelling view that crosses a near silhouette, a view close enough to read its action, and a final stillness. Pick the number and order of shots for the story.

Cut each view from six to nine planes at different distances. Put transmitted blue or violet light through the far cards. Use a family of related tones in each place, shifting hue when the location or mood changes. Give the far hills, roofs or organic forms small hand-cut interior contours. Overlap staggered planes so the viewer can follow the gap by the falling shadow. Draw the darkest card across the bottom. Leave breathing room around the principal action, including when a caption appears. A near plane may pass across the glass, but the story plane should stay in focus.

Use small amber light sources with a bright centre, soft fringe and a reason to turn on or off. When it is important that one object illuminates another, draw the receiving surface or reflection. Attach a restrained sound to the change. Pivot around a hinge or contact, including its moving shadow. Reveal or conceal with card, depth, a short dissolve or a motivated interruption of the light. A calm cut is stronger than a second camera move when the light is already changing. End on an object particular to the film; if a title is needed, letter a physical paper prop and allow time to read it.

Write and record English lines with space for sound and action. Place warm off-white serif captions over the darkest card. Write the score to the picture, with breathing room for water, paper, footsteps or switches. Review the travelling silhouette at native speed and the emissive details and edge separation at delivery size. Dark shots need a clearly readable midtone. Listen for whether the featured action makes its sound at the actual moment of contact.

For the midnight treatment, compare a small image of the widest shot with the lights switched on and off. The sky and far card should still read as night in both. In a finished frame about two thirds of the image may sit below 30/255 luma; a sunlit-looking window covering the upper half destroys the illusion. Keep the body of a brass object in cool half-light, with amber on its working rim, eye or perforation. Give a tracking camera enough physical distance for a near cutout to enter, cross and leave; use the distant scenery to judge how far the camera moved. At a mechanical contact, clear the moving joint of any decorative strip. For plaque lettering, test a delivery-size frame with the illumination on.

For orchestral cues, a real plucked-harp transient and a sustained recorded wind or string can share the original note timeline. The kit accepts licensed pitched samples in audio/instruments/map.json. Keep the original sample origins, recording licences and replayable master with the film.
