# Tabletop modelling-clay comedy

Put the lens at the height of a small clay performer. Let an everyday object loom large and change the course of the scene. The audience should be able to follow the predicament with the sound off. Give each character a different way of handling the same object. Build the action through an attempt, a visible consequence, a reaction and a changed plan.

Sculpt a slightly asymmetric pear-shaped trunk, tapering neck, bent arms, planted broad feet, individually directed eyes, a separate projecting nose and a carved mouth. Keep the head attached as it turns. Give pressed surfaces a faint whorl and little glints. Stage the action on a real-looking wood surface under one large raking lamp. Place practical studio objects behind the performers at a few separate depths. Compose a close-up, busy two-shot, isolated prop insert and a payoff wide. Use longer lenses for faces. Keep unimportant scenery out of focus. An original photographed or generated empty background may be mapped onto the far wall. Keep all action and its shadows in the 3D scene.

Use twos for quiet acting on a 25 fps timeline. Use an eye dart before the head turn, anticipation before a shove, a quick change of balance, an overshoot, and a readable settle. Differentiate the timing of each performer. Let a foot retain its world position during the plant. Tie hand targets to the object's world position while it is held. A dialogue line can be very short. Leave silence for the prop, with a small score and hand-timed Foley expressing its material.

For a reference comparison, decode both sources to their last frames, keep shot roles and title/brand intervals, pair matching shot roles in both directions and look at the worst matches. Recheck the light on clay, finger and foot contact, face performance, set dressing and lens behavior. Save the independent first encode before comparison when running a skill transfer.

On a carry, mark the point that bears the load. Keep the thumb and opposing pads against that surface until another hand or support takes over. Focus the portrait on the near eye and the prop insert on the contact. Map the timber grain to the tabletop in world coordinates and keep the actor legible against the back wall. Inspect the carved cavity and lower lip with the mouth both open and closed.

Block the important reaction with the whole silhouette. Give a hand or hip a destination tied to the object, then start the head, torso and feet on slightly different exposures. Hold the achieved pose long enough to read. Check adjacent poses around the fastest movement and in the following settle.

For a tightly shut eye, lift the cheek and sculpt two flesh-coloured lids into the head. Carve the meeting seam across a rounded replacement lid. Fan the short pressure creases from the nasal corner. Let the lid cover the eye gradually during closure and reveal it again on the next exposures. Check the half-closed exposure for a detached brow. Pinch the lips towards a small opening on an effort pose. For press-and-release action, inspect the deepest compression from the taking lens. Track the palm on its target and leave enough face clearance to read the prop.

A prop can hide a correctly aimed hand. View the contact from the camera and place the bent elbow, palm and individual pads where their outline can be read. Let the soft surface compress under the pads and spring back after release. Check the contact at full resolution across consecutive exposures.

For dialogue, mark the actual take's phoneme boundaries. Hold the lips together on M, B and P, raise the lower lip for F and V, and vary the mouth width and jaw for vowels. Preserve at least one exposure for a short closure. Check a close-up with the actual take. Pendular arm movement can leave a two-shot looking stiff: shift the pelvis between independently planted soles, let one foot rise, direct the eyes toward that foot and delay the second character's reaction. Check that both faces remain legible at their deepest lean.

For practical sets, photograph or generate enough of the empty room to cover the entire travelling camera. Inspect the edge of the widest view for doubled props or stretched pixels. Use an overhead wood plate on the table. On metal, retain the local wood and wall colours, the bright rectangular light and the dark unlit side, and soften the transition where the reflected table ends. Follow the moving highlights through a gesture and check where a fingertip or soft cover blocks the light.

For a leap, pose both soles in world space. Mark the last push, both feet clear, the first foot down and the second foot down. Give the body a different outline on each of those exposures. Carry the hips through a low recovery before the next step. Keep the camera wide enough to see the head, the foot on the ground and the pool of shadow. Time each footfall to its own contact. If a clay shape transforms, anchor the emerging mass at its point of support and review its shadow throughout the size change.

For a tactile close-up, build the clay surface from a smooth oil-clay swatch. Keep most of the face quiet and let a few pressure marks catch the light. Mount the relief on the actor, then turn the head in a locked camera to check that the marks follow the clay. Let the meeting edge of a pressed eyelid bend towards the nose and vary the thickness of the two lids. Use separate soft upper and lower pads around a compressed mouth. Check the silhouette of the opening at the final picture size.

For a conversation across the table, place both lenses on the same side of the line joining the actors. Leave enough room in each reverse for the speaking face while the listener turns or walks. Check the near, middle and final poses; where a foreground head crosses, check every intervening exposure. Give the eyes a physical target on the partner or prop. Delay the head and shoulder turn a few exposures. Let the thumb oppose the curled pads at the actual grip and preserve a gap between the hand-held prop and the torso. Inspect the contact in the taking camera.

Compare the lit and unlit cheek, the eye white and the painted pupil in an actual portrait from the picture stream. Keep the pupil's brown pigment distinct from the dark mouth. When adding fill to a face, let the chin and hat brim interrupt it. Check a wide frame as well as the close-up before accepting the light.

Press each eye into the head and leave a narrow contact shadow where the light is blocked. Review it with the head turned both ways. As the eyelid covers the eye, recede the exposed seat beneath it. Bring the cheek into the nose during a tight squeeze. Soften the edge of an open mouth in the cavity itself, and reduce the rounding as the lips shut. Check the deepest visible part of the mouth on the actual speech exposures for M, B and P.

On a reply in close-up, check the focus through the deepest lean. Track focus to the actual near eye after the head has been posed, including blink exposures. The foreground listener and the prop may fall outside the focal plane.

In a shared shot, let the listener do something that changes the speaker's next move. Pose the approach, first touch, supported hold and release. Choose a view from which the audience can see the space opening between the two surfaces. Check both faces at the deepest reach. Give each performer a different first reaction exposure and sync the impact sound to the first frame of contact.

When a third performer enters, show their purpose and stage the observers’ reactions at different times. Block the walk around the depth of the set. Check the approach from above, then from the taking lens.

For an animal at rest, look at an observed pose of that species. Block the spine, tucked limbs, head support and tail together before modelling small features. Read the silhouette from the taking lens with the set and bedding in place. At a noise, animate the degree of response the story calls for. A sleeper can stir with a breath or an ear twitch. Carry the same rest pose and eye state through the wide, insert and return shot.

When a sleeper handles a prop, let the paw locate a real grip, transfer the head’s weight to the support and then relax the grip. Check the jaw, nose and paws at the working scale. At delivery size, identify each conspicuous patch, dot and edge on the body. Toggle the body, textile and trim separately if a stitch or intersecting surface reads as an anatomical mark. Rework its attachment or omit the detail and inspect the neighbouring frames in the encoded cut.

For a held squeeze, watch when the reference pose arrives, how long it stays and whether it reopens before the cut. A deep upper lid should roll over the lower pad near the nose; let the seam narrow toward the temple. Turn the head through the light and inspect both eyes, the bridge, the chin and the reopening. A held face can still turn slightly while the lids keep their pressure.

Tie the lower jaw to a hinge below the ear and keep the neck pivot behind the mouth. In a talk or a downward look, compare a render with the neck against the identical exposure with only the neck hidden. Inspect any difference inside the mouth. Move the offending joint until the oral cavity remains clear throughout the lean. For a sealed consonant use a pressed shallow crease. With the jaw open, let the two corners pull at different heights and the chin drop while the upper lip stays near the nose. Check the side view for a detached ridge on the far lip. Sample the eye and cheek in the same light as the reference and inspect their highlights and shadows separately.

View the two squeezed faces at the same head width. If the camera directions differ, mirror a clearly labeled still for the placement check and keep the original moving reference beside it. Locate the top of the nose relative to the inner lid corners. Press the bridge up between them. Let the upper lip carry more clay than the lower roll. A peaked aperture keeps a tiny opening legible in three-quarter view. Inspect a normal blink and a spoken rounded vowel beside the full squeeze.

For a held squeeze, follow the inner and outer eye corners across each exposure. Register close crops to those corners to compare the fold as the head turns. Check the lit upper pad, the shaded face beside the nose and the shadow cast onto the cheek separately. A soft area lamp and light returned from the tabletop can make the lower rim legible. Inspect both temples at the native camera resolution through a turn, a half-open lid and reopening. Check any deeper engraving in profile. If the lines read as a drawn symbol, soften or shorten the tool marks and judge them again in the complete frame. If a character examines a fixed object, the shoulders can finish turning while the face stays on it.

When several official edits are available, compare them with one another before rating the production. Hold out a contiguous stretch of the full episode and keep every exposure of a camera setup on the same side of the comparison. Check distributions by shot size or story function and repeat with the same number of shots as the new film. Try a deliberately frozen or desaturated counterexample. A nearby official still can also match a frozen performance. Inspect any outlier at its action cue and document the sensitivity of the measurement.

Place an ordinary blink on the actual exposure clock. Give it one or two completely closed poses between readable open eyes. Keep a deliberately narrowed or drowsy eye as a separate acting choice. In a turn or a shared frame, inspect the eyes at the taking camera's native resolution on the approach, full closure and release. A blink in the score can disappear between exposures; a fully closed pose can also disappear behind a prop. Check both.

For a compressed clay lid, start the upper, middle and lower strokes at slightly different positions. Let pressure fall away at the tip of a light stroke and give the displaced clay a small raised edge. Turn the cast past the same light and look for the point where a shallow stroke vanishes. Check the groove at its own scale so a large lighting bias does not flatten the mark.

## Timing, score and Foley

Edit on one 25 fps timeline. Mark the start and end of each spoken take, the first visible contact, the reaction and the return to rest. Shorten dead time between these beats while protecting the actor’s recorded tempo and the pause that sells the joke. Let the story set the running time. Hold quiet acting on twos. For fast events, sample the rig on ones through first contact and recovery. Recheck both sides of a cut and watch the whole sequence at normal speed.

For a new score, use Gemini to generate an instrumental cue shaped to the scene. Keep the source recording, generation details and credits. Cut at phrase boundaries and lower the score under English dialogue. Leave a pocket around a key contact. Spot recorded Foley against the output exposure and shape its loudness, stereo position and decay for the distance and material. A cover that damps a resonant object should change the tail as well as the level. Audition the encoded mix at ordinary speed and keep the dialogue, music and Foley stems. In a picture-only revision, retain the accepted takes, music and effects.

## Prop contact and character revisions

Write down what supports each prop during the reach, supported hold, release, free motion and settling. Derive every linked part from the same input and rest position. For a launched prop, resolve its flight from release velocity and gravity through the first supporting surface. Check the landing shape and residual motion before attaching it to a moving support. Use these same events for the Foley cues.

Check the complete moving shape against the fingers, palm, torso, clothing and set on each exposed frame. Test local surfaces at maximum reach, deepest compression, release and both sides of an edit. Place the camera where the grip, contact shadow and separation are visible. Review failed contacts again in the final encode at delivery size and normal speed.

Create original puppets with a simple round head, a slightly asymmetric body and a readable silhouette. For a colour and shape revision, begin with the chosen sculpt, change the clay swatch, then adjust only the head width or nose projection that needs it. Preserve the eyes, mouth, accessories and acting. Compare matched poses with the same lens, light and scale, including profile and the tightest prop contact. Check hat brims, seated eyes and lip seals on the revised shape. When a specific real-world identity, flag or brand belongs in the brief, use its identifying features and colours in the miniature treatment.
